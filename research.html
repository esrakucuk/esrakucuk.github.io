Research

How does a mind acquire a concept it did not begin with? I work on two concepts, the possible and the living, and on the period before children can tell you what they know, because that is where the answer has to be visible if it is anywhere.

One problem runs through all of my work. When a young child fails a task, has she not yet acquired the concept, or does she have it and lack the resources the task happens to demand? Separating these is the through-line of the projects below.

Children’s concepts of possibility

What can young children represent as merely possible?

In a classic task, children must place a hand under each exit of a tube to be sure of catching a ball. Four-year-olds do this reliably and 3-year-olds typically do not, which has been taken as evidence that younger children cannot hold mutually exclusive possibilities in mind.

I asked whether these failures reflect a missing concept or the demands of representing possibilities about location. In a new paradigm the uncertainty is about identity: two animals take turns going down a slide, and children get a snack ready for whichever animal comes out. Three-year-olds prepared for both possibilities as well as 4-year-olds did (Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, 2025). When I matched identity and location tasks directly, 2- to 4-year-olds were more accurate under identity uncertainty across the whole age range (Open Mind, 2026).

My dissertation argues that this asymmetry comes from the object representational system. Object indexes represent an object’s location necessarily but bind identifying features only optionally, so an index can hold several possible identities open for one object but cannot place one object in two locations.

Does the timing of uncertainty matter?

A ball that has not yet dropped leaves the world open, while a ball that has already landed leaves only the child’s knowledge incomplete. Competing accounts predict opposite developmental orders. With matched future and present tasks, 3- and 4-year-olds performed similarly in both contexts (Developmental Psychology, 2026). A Bayesian meta-analysis across the literature is under review.

What limits performance in possibility tasks?

Preparing for two outcomes requires representing the outcomes and also the actions that would cover them. When 3-year-olds were first shown the set of possible actions, they recognized and adopted the optimal strategy as well as 4-year-olds (Cognition, 2024). Related work with Fatima Tuba Yaylacı asks how active participation changes children’s reasoning about possibility, including in Turkish children (under review).

With Stephanie Alderete, Fei Xu, and Melissa Kibbe, I compared the five tasks that dominate this literature on five ancillary demands: physical reasoning, action planning, working memory, language, and temporal context. We argue that children represent fewer possibilities as demands rise, so the minimal competence often attributed to 3-year-olds is a resource signature rather than a conceptual stage (Developmental Psychology, 2026).

planningtask demands

Where do possibilities come from?

Every task in this literature hands children the alternatives to consider. In ongoing work with Elizabeth Bonawitz at Harvard, 3- to 6-year-olds choose which windows to look through to learn about a hidden shape and draw what might be behind one, giving a measure of possibility reasoning that does not depend on stating a disjunction aloud.

Children’s concepts of what is alive

On a standard account, children first build a category of living things from cues to agency, such as self-propelled motion and goal-directedness. Plants are the crucial test case: they are living, yet they lack nearly every such cue. If children hold an earlier commitment about what plants are, it should show up in what they expect and project before it shows up in what they say. I test this at three points in development.

Do infants represent plants as a distinct kind?

With Melissa Kibbe, I adapted an object individuation paradigm to ask whether prelinguistic infants treat plants as a kind apart from agents and inanimate objects. This is a registered report in preparation.

infancyindividuation

Do children expect plants to have animal-like insides?

School-age children judge whether animals, plants, and non-living natural objects are alive, and predict what each has inside. The question is whether their expectations about plants go beyond what they will explicitly call alive.

Does being alive confer moral standing?

With Deborah Kelemen at Boston University, I ask whether 4- to 7-year-olds extend moral concern along the same boundary, and whether plants sit between people and rocks in a child’s moral hierarchy. Manuscript under review.

Where this is going

Three lines follow from the work so far: testing whether the identity advantage is present in infancy with looking-time measures, studying how the breadth of a hypothesis space shapes what children imagine and where they look for information, and asking whether the profile of what children treat as alive, rather than the label they give, predicts the moral standing they grant living things.
