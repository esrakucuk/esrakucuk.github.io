Teaching philosophy

Learning, at any age, means entertaining ideas that might be wrong, sitting productively with uncertainty, and testing one’s thinking against evidence. I build classrooms where “I don’t know yet” is the beginning of inquiry.

I make reasoning visible. I teach psychology as a record of questions, competing possibilities, and the studies designed to decide between them. Before revealing a classic result, I ask students to generate the possible outcomes and what each would imply.

I lower the cost of being wrong. I use frequent low-stakes structures such as brief written predictions, think-pair-share, and drafts that receive feedback before grades attach, so that error counts as information.

I teach the skills, not just the content. Statistical reasoning, experimental design, and scientific writing are the durable outcomes of a psychology degree, so every course I teach has students design, critique, analyze, and write.

As a first-generation college student and an international scholar, I scaffold vocabulary explicitly, offer several ways to take part (writing, small groups, whole-class discussion), and treat accommodations as part of ordinary good design.
